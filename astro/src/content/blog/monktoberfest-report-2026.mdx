Monktoberfest is one of my favorite conferences to attend. Fresh off the plane, I wanted to share why this is one of the conferences I don't miss if I can help it.

{/* more */}

## It's far off the beaten track

It happens in Portland, Maine, in the fall. For folks coming from anywhere west of Chicago or across the Atlantic, there are no direct flights. You can take two flights, or one flight and a bus ride, or one flight and a train ride.

This means travel is a total pain in the butt, but people there really want to be there. [Monktoberfest](https://monktoberfest.com/) happens for three days every fall, and it's put on by the [good folks at RedMonk](https://redmonk.com/), a boutique analyst firm focused on developers. The developer-focused ethos runs through the whole conference, from the talk topics to the way sponsors are handled to the plentiful coffee to the 10:00 a.m. start time.

If you do attend, plan to arrive mid-day Wednesday so you can join the harbor tour. It's super touristy but a fun way to step into conversations while seeing some of the beauty of Portland. And many folks take the whole week and work remotely, or extend their trip over the weekend after.

## It's discrete

You don't know the agenda or the speakers until you show up. They're even secretive about when tickets go on sale. This is relatively unique among tech conferences, which are usually pretty upfront about what attendees can expect.

It adds an air of serendipity and exclusivity, but also fosters community. Similiar to the hike to get there, the fact you have to actively pursue tickets without any idea of the speakers means you have to want to go for other reasons, such as the reputation and the other attendees.

One of the organizers described it as [what Rands would call a "welcome to our home" type of conference](https://randsinrepose.com/archives/building-serendipity/), where part of the point is, as Rands' article mentions: "each conference is a reflection of the care of these organizers".

## There are no sponsor booths

I once talked to our events person about FusionAuth about sponsoring Monktoberfest. Her first question was, "How many leads do you think we'd get?" The answer is zero. Zero leads. There are no booths, just a sponsor shelf with a minimal swag. There's no badge scanning, no spectacle and no prizes. Sponsors are thanked multiple times and have their logo displayed. But it is 100% a brand-halo conference for sponsors, not a conference where you interact with people and get leads. 

The answer from the events person was basically "Wow, sounds cool. But we can't do this out of my budget." Fair!

For attendees, the minimal sponsorship is a benefit. You focus on interaction with other attendees and the talks. I know how important vendors are for conference sustainability and I take time to visit them. You can absolutely gain useful information by visiting the sponsor area at conferences and chatting with vendors. Some folks find sponsor conversations energizing and useful, and others find them distracting draining. Monktoberfest caters to the second kind of person.

That said, one of the sponsors of this year's conference was a surprise and definitely improved my view of them. And I wasn't alone. The brand halo effect can work, it's just a long long play that needs to be backed up by actions as well as money.

## The food and beer are amazing

This isn't just a conference about tech. It's also about beer and food, and both are fantastic. Lunch was a nice but typical conference lunch.

The dinner is another story. Each time I've attended, it's been a buffet of amazing food, beautifully presented. I'm talking sushi, oysters, fancy grits, tacos and more.

There's also a beer expert at dinner. Ryan shows up with a long list of beers, all interesting and many local. The line to get a beer is long, but that's because he's willing to take time and offer you advice on what you should get, based on your desires. He's almost a beer sommelier, and it's unlike typical conferences or events. There's also wine, but I didn't check that out.

I chatted with an attendee who didn't drink and he said there were also non-alcoholic options such as seltzer and soda.

## The attendees are great

I think there are two categories of attendees: first-timers from the local area, first times from elsewhere and regulars who use the conference as a reunion.

The local first-timers bring a less technical point of view to the conference, even though they are often involved with tech. This year I talked for a while with a non-technical founder who was from Maine.

That last group can make things a little awkward; I'll be honest. The bonds between people who've attended Monktoberfest many times and shared experiences can be off-putting to a newcomer. But if I fight my introvert nature and strike up conversations, most people are super welcoming and happy to chat. There's also a slack where attendees who don't quite know what is going on can see after-hours activities.

Since this was my second time, I felt more comfortable. I also caught up with some former co-workers or attendees that I'd met last time and interacted with online since.

I've had excellent conversations about technical leaders' thoughts on industry and technologies. It's often the on-the-ground perspective about what is working and what isn't that you don't always get at other conferences because conversation is explicitly fostered. The long breaks and dinner organization augment that.

## The talks

Monktoberfest is a single-track conference, and the talks are technosocial in nature. 

What does that fancy word mean? You'll hear talks that interest nerds and technologists, but they won't be super tactical or immediately applicable. No topics like "check out the latest cool library for Rust" or "when should you consider JMS vs Kafka". They're thought-provoking bigger-picture talks instead, tying technical concerns to wider perspectives, such as health, ethics or society.

To give you a taste of the conference talks, topics this year included burnout, what technologists owe others when they build certain types of technology, educating the next generation of developers, endometriosis and what towns in Maine can teach us about identity. While it is hard to pick, I had two favorite talks this year.

One was an impressive, funny rant about systemic complexity and the abstractions that give software such power (I know, I know). The other was about community efforts to build a [bike bus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bike_bus) for schoolchildren can teach SREs about managing and improving legacy systems at work.

As far as I can tell, there isn't really a conference theme other than "be thought-provoking".
If I had to pick one takeaway from this year, it's that AI is being recognized as a powerful tool for building software but not a cure-all. Just as there's a backlash in the USA against data centers and the overweening aspects of AI companies, I saw similar sentiment. There was discussion of the many ways AI can be helpful as well as ways it can be problematic and even distressing.

## Should you go?

If you're looking for a conference in a remote part of the United States, you want your mind bent a little, and you'd like interesting conversations with people from tiny startups and giant companies like Oracle, AWS and Google, Monktoberfest is a good fit.
